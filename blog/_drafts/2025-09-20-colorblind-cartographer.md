Last night was the first big snow of the season in Vermont. In my city the leaves turned late and this snowfall landed early, giving us the rare gift of "snowliage". As I walked to work this morning I came upon two people separately stopped on the sidewalk to take photos of an overhanging Japanese Maple tree, resplendant with snow caps over a full set of leaves. They marveled at how it looked, and I knew then that the tree was in a deep red hue at the onset of winter. I took no photos. I smiled and walked on. 

The leaves to me looked dark gray or brown, approaching black. I could not see what the others saw.

<hr>

My wife worked as a babysitter on the side back when we first started dating, minding some single-digit-aged munchkins. At one point she mentioned that her boyfriend (👋) was colorblind, and they asked, adorably, "Does he use a cane?"

The answer of course is that I don't, but that story illustrates the not-quite-a-disability confusion of the fact that I am a [proud?] Protanope. Like 2% of the male population, [I am fully missing the red-perceiving cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_blindness#Red%E2%80%93green_color_blindness) in what is supposed to be a three-channel sensor system in my eyes. As a result, I mostly can't see any difference in hue between a rose and the leaves around it, and Cookie Monster and Grimace appear to be the exact same color to me. This is not the world's biggest headache for the most part, except for when I'm in the woods, determining the safety of berries based on how closely they resemble gummy bears.

![2](/shoals/assets/img/2025-09-20_2.jpg)

_GTG, right?_

However, some among you will have already detected a major issue, and perhaps asked yourselves: __"How does this deficiency affect his occupation?"__

I am here to tell you that I am a colorblind cartographer, and I am not alone.

## What I do about it

This is the _tiniest_ of oversimplifications, but color in the digital sphere is basically just numbers. 

<hr>

I've spent most of my life, perhaps not celebrating, but at least appreciating the difference of my perspective. I know that my world looks different from everyone else's, but it's never felt like a less than. But in working on this piece I finally, earnestly solicited some opinions from friends and colleagues about what they saw in the same images that I was looking at - in particular in the comparison between the true color image of the airstrip above with the protonopia-simulation version next to it. The words I heard to describe my simulated view of the world were "leached", "desaturated", "sepia". This was honestly shocking, and perhaps the first hint I've had since I was very young that the world might be more vibrant than I could ever imagine.
