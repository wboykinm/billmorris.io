Last night, my son, while driving(!) to a friend's house with me as permit-supervisor, remarked that the cars in [Cyberpunk 2077](https://web.archive.org/web/20260119205516/https://kotaku.com/cyberpunk-2077-the-kotaku-review-1845946628) seemed like realistic ideas of what cars would be fifty years from now. With that offhand glance at a video game's timeline, the scope of history's march and the human lifespan entered the chat. No, dominated it, and took over my brain logs after he had dropped himself off and I drove home in silence.

<hr>

This morning I resumed reading Niall Williams' novel [_This is Happiness_](https://niallwilliams.com/pages/this-is-happiness), in which nothing much happens that you might later relate, but wherein every observation and thought lingers in its manner of telling. However, one of the Nothing Muches sounds momentous in a vacuum: a village in rural Western Ireland experiences the coming of electrification in the early 1950s. Outsiders arrive for the planning and for the work, equipment is unspooled, and a simple notion is broached for every household, of bringing light by wire instead of by flame.

I was born into switches and plugs. Though I was raised in one of the last Vermont towns to be added to the grid, the work had been completed thirty years prior, in roughly the same era as that fictional Irish village. But at the unfurling of the 21st century I was living in a very real village in the Dominican mountains, where no roads or wires reached, but where the locals had electrified with mushroomed photovoltaic panels hooked to car batteries hooked to single bare bulbs under which men would slap dominos down hard onto card tables until the charge ran out and the darkness resumed. I was there to help as outlying households were brought into this gridless club, and made the move from guttering flame to incandescence. I delivered such equipment to shacks in Northern Haiti, expanding the evening glow there in the same sort of manifest promise with which Edison and Westinghouse had bragged their way to the illumination of Manhattan a century earlier.

It's not that the folks in any of these places had never seen electric light before. No, they had been steeped in the knowledge of it, and I imagine the hundred-year gap in the technology's arrival was suffered as not the smallest of Haiti's injustices. But I'll mangle someone else's wisdom here to remind us all that the future is always already present, just unevenly distributed. The villagers in Trou-du-Nord had at one time or another been under the patchy streetlights of Cap-Haitien, and had watched the evening programs on a neighbor's television. I once spoke with a man in Norfolk, England, who told me that his village had neither electricity nor indoor plumbing when [my Grandfather's USAAF bomber squadron](https://billmorris.io/shoals/2020/03/09/chanel-number-5.html) arrived in 1943, looming out of the clouds in massive stainless steel war machines, alighting onto airstrips that had been summoned out of the fallow fields. "It was like the landing of aliens."

<hr>

In our conversation last night, I told my son I didn't expect to be around to verify what cars would look like in 2077. He reminded me that we have "good genetics" for longevity. He's right, of course. His great-grandmother experienced the electrification of Philadelphia, then observed every slow-coming technological distribution up to the arrival of the Large Language Model. My son met her before she died, and so the span of two overlapping lifetimes in our family will encompass the Ford Model T all the way up to whatever the hell it is that gets us around at the onset of the 22nd century. 

What a wonder, to watch it all happen.
